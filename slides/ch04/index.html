One sample, a whole population

Act I · The tension

This is the chapter on which every later chapter rests. Every regression table in the book reports a standard error, a t statistic, a p-value and a confidence interval, and all four are introduced here for the simplest possible estimator: the sample mean. If students master the logic for one mean, the regression chapters only change the estimator, not the reasoning.

We measure a sample, but we care about the population

We surveyed 171 women aged 30 who work full time and found mean earnings of $41,413. We do not care about these 171 people as such. We care about all 30-year-old women working full time in the U.S. in 2010.

The sample mean \(\bar{x}\) is our best guess of the population mean \(\mu\), but it is only one draw: another sample of 171 would give a different number. Statistical inference turns that single number into a statement with quantified uncertainty, either a range (a confidence interval) or a verdict on a claim (a hypothesis test).

The whole chapter answers one question: how far from \(\mu\) could \(\bar{x}\) plausibly be?

Inference is the bridge from the sample we have to the population we want to describe. Stress that the population mean is a fixed, unknown number; what is random is the sample, and therefore the sample mean and every interval built from it.

One formula carries the whole chapter

\[t \;=\; \frac{\text{estimate} \;-\; \text{hypothesized value}}{\text{standard error}} \qquad\qquad \text{CI} \;=\; \text{estimate} \;\pm\; \text{critical value} \times \text{standard error}\]

  • The estimate here is \(\bar{x}\) (or \(\hat{p}\) for a proportion); in Chapter 7 it becomes a regression slope \(b_2\)
  • The standard error measures how much the estimate would vary across repeated samples
  • The critical value comes from the \(t\) distribution (or the normal for proportions)

Learn this pair of formulas once and you can read every inference table in the book.

Key Takeaway 9 of the chapter: the methods generalize to other parameters. Point forward explicitly: in Chapter 7 the same t = (b − β*)/se(b) and b ± t × se(b) appear in every regression output.

Seven ideas anchor this chapter

  • \(H_0\) vs \(H_a\): the default claim we test, and the alternative we accept if it falls
  • Test statistic \(t\): how many standard errors the estimate sits from the value in \(H_0\)
  • \(p\)-value: the probability of data at least this extreme if \(H_0\) were true
  • Significance level \(\alpha\): the false-alarm rate we accept, usually \(0.05\)
  • Critical value: the cutoff on \(t\) that bounds the rejection region
  • Type I / Type II error: a false alarm vs a missed effect; power \(= 1-\beta\)
  • Confidence interval: the range of \(\mu\) values a two-sided test would not reject

A test never proves \(H_0\). It only asks whether the data can reject it.

These are the chapter’s Key Concepts glossary. Spend time on the p-value definition: it is a probability about the data computed under H0, never the probability that H0 is true.

Section 4.1 · How precise is one sample mean?

Sample mean · standard deviation · standard error

Section 4.1: the motivating example. Tools: the sample mean, the sample standard deviation, and the standard error.

The question: what do 30-year-old women earn?

Data. AED_EARNINGS.DTA: 171 women aged 30 working full time in the U.S. in 2010, annual earnings in dollars.

Symbol Name Measures
\(\bar{x}\) sample mean our point estimate of the population mean \(\mu\)
\(s\) sample standard deviation how much individual earnings vary
\(\text{se}(\bar{x}) = s/\sqrt{n}\) standard error how much the sample mean would vary across samples

Two different spreads: \(s\) describes people; the standard error describes our estimate.

The data are a sample from a much larger population. The chapter’s Key Concept 4.1: the standard error measures the precision of x̄ as an estimate of μ. Students regularly confuse s and se; this table is worth pausing on.

Earnings are right-skewed: a few high earners pull the mean up

Earnings of the 171 women. The mean ($41,413) sits above the median ($36,000).

64% of the women earn less than the mean. Inference here is about the mean, which is not the typical earner.

Figure computed for these slides from the chapter’s data. Earnings run from $1,050 to $172,000; skewness 1.71. With n = 171 the central limit theorem makes the sampling distribution of the mean close to normal even though earnings are skewed, so t-based inference is still reasonable. With very small samples from a skewed population it would not be.

Code: load the data and compute the standard error

import numpy as np                          # square roots
import pandas as pd                         # tabular data
from scipy import stats                     # t and normal distributions (used later)
url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quarcs-lab/data-open/master/AED/AED_EARNINGS.DTA"
data_earnings = pd.read_stata(url)          # 171 women aged 30, full-time, 2010
earnings = data_earnings["earnings"]        # annual earnings in dollars
n = len(earnings)                           # sample size
mean_earnings = earnings.mean()             # sample mean x-bar
std_earnings = earnings.std(ddof=1)         # sample std dev s (divides by n - 1)
se_earnings = std_earnings / np.sqrt(n)     # standard error = s / sqrt(n)
print(f"n = {n}")                           # 171
print(f"Mean: ${mean_earnings:,.2f}")       # point estimate of mu
print(f"Std dev: ${std_earnings:,.2f}")     # spread across women
print(f"Std error: ${se_earnings:,.2f}")    # spread of x-bar across samples

Individual earnings vary by about $25,500, but the average of 171 of them varies by only about $1,950.

The SE is 13 times smaller than s because √171 ≈ 13.1. With 43 women the SE would roughly double, to $3,893: not exactly 2 × 1,952 = 3,904, because 43 is slightly more than 171/4. In general, quartering the sample doubles the SE and quadrupling it halves the SE.

Reading the standard error

  • Repeated-sampling meaning: if we drew many samples of 171 women, their means would typically differ from \(\mu\) by about $1,950
  • Precision relative to the mean: $1,952 is under 5% of $41,413, so the estimate is reasonably precise
  • Square-root law: precision grows with \(\sqrt{n}\), so halving the SE costs four times the data

Key Concept 4.1: the standard error is the single number behind every interval and test that follows.

The square-root law is why surveys get expensive: going from ±$3,900 to ±$1,950 needs 4× the sample, and to ±$975 needs 16×.

What the standard error assumes

It requires

  • a random sample: each woman is an independent draw from the population
  • the formula \(s/\sqrt{n}\) is built on independence

It does not capture

  • non-sampling error: misreported earnings, non-response
  • dependence: clustered or time-series data need other SEs (Ch. 7, 12, 17)

The SE measures sampling noise only, and only under independence. GDP growth in 4.5 will show what happens when independence fails.

Standard caveat. The formula is derived for independent draws. Survey data with clusters, or time series where this quarter resembles the last, violate it, and the s/√n formula then understates the real uncertainty.

Section 4.2 · Why a \(t\) ruler, not a normal one?

\(t\) statistic · degrees of freedom · fat tails

Section 4.2: the t statistic and the t distribution. Tools: standardizing the sample mean, degrees of freedom, critical values.

The \(t\) statistic standardizes the sample mean

\[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\text{se}(\bar{x})} = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \;\sim\; T(n-1)\]

  • If we knew \(\sigma\), \((\bar{x}-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})\) would be standard normal
  • We estimate \(\sigma\) by \(s\), and that extra uncertainty fattens the tails: the \(t\) distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom
  • Exact when the population is normal; otherwise a good approximation for moderate \(n\) (central limit theorem)

Fatter tails mean bigger critical values: the price of not knowing \(\sigma\).

Key Concept 4.2. The degrees of freedom are n − 1 because one degree of freedom is used to estimate the mean before computing s.

The \(t\) distribution approaches the normal as \(n\) grows

Panel A: \(t(4)\) has much fatter tails than the standard normal. Panel B: \(t(30)\) is already close to it.

With 4 degrees of freedom, values beyond ±2 are over twice as likely as under the normal.

Chapter Figure 4.1. Numbers computed for these slides: P(|T| > 2) is 11.6% for t(4), 5.5% for t(30) and 4.6% for the standard normal.

from scipy import stats                     # t and normal distributions

    t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df)         # 97.5th percentile: two-sided 5% cutoff

Section 4.3 · What values of \(\mu\) are plausible?

Confidence intervals · margin of error · the confidence–precision trade-off

Section 4.3: confidence intervals. Tools: the CI formula, the correct repeated-sampling interpretation, and the trade-off between confidence and width.

A confidence interval is estimate ± margin of error

\[\bar{x} \;\pm\; t_{n-1,\,\alpha/2} \times \text{se}(\bar{x})\]

  • \(t_{n-1,\,\alpha/2}\): the critical value; for 95% confidence, \(\alpha = 0.05\) and we leave 2.5% in each tail
  • Margin of error \(= t_{n-1,\,\alpha/2} \times \text{se}(\bar{x})\)
  • Meaning: if we repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain \(\mu\)

The interval is random; \(\mu\) is fixed. The 95% describes the method, not this one interval.

Key Concept 4.3. The phrase “we are 95% confident” is shorthand for the repeated-sampling property. Rule of thumb for n > 30: x̄ ± 2 × se.

Code: the 95% confidence interval for mean earnings

import numpy as np                          # square roots
import pandas as pd                         # tabular data
from scipy import stats                     # t distribution
url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quarcs-lab/data-open/master/AED/AED_EARNINGS.DTA"
earnings = pd.read_stata(url)["earnings"]   # 171 women, annual earnings
n = len(earnings)                           # sample size
mean_earnings = earnings.mean()             # x-bar
se_earnings = earnings.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)    # s / sqrt(n)
conf_level = 0.95                           # confidence level
alpha = 1 - conf_level                      # 0.05
t_crit = stats.t.ppf(1 - alpha/2, n - 1)    # critical value t(170, 0.025)
margin_of_error = t_crit * se_earnings      # half-width of the interval
print(mean_earnings - margin_of_error, mean_earnings + margin_of_error)  # the CI

Reading the interval right

Correct

  • 95% of intervals built this way, across repeated samples, contain \(\mu\)
  • “We are 95% confident \(\mu\) is between $37,559 and $45,266”, as shorthand for the above
  • The interval reflects sampling uncertainty only

Wrong

  • “There is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is in this interval”
  • “95% of women earn between $37,559 and $45,266”
  • “The interval contains 95% of the data”

The interval is about the mean. Individual earnings range from $1,050 to $172,000.

The second wrong reading is the most common in student work. Contrast the CI width (~$7,700) with the standard deviation of individual earnings (~$25,500).

    alpha = 1 - conf                        # tail probability, split in two

Section 4.4 · Is \(\mu = \$40{,}000\) credible?

Two-sided test · \(p\)-value · critical value · Type I and II errors · power

Section 4.4: the two-sided hypothesis test. Tools: H0 and Ha, the test statistic, the two equivalent decision rules, the duality with the confidence interval, the two kinds of error, and power.

A hypothesis test asks whether a claim survives the data

\[H_0: \mu = \mu_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_a: \mu \neq \mu_0 \qquad\qquad t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\text{se}(\bar{x})} \sim T(n-1) \text{ if } H_0 \text{ is true}\]

  • \(p\)-value rule: \(p = 2 \times \Pr[T_{n-1} \geq |t|]\); reject \(H_0\) if \(p < \alpha\)
  • Critical-value rule: reject \(H_0\) if \(|t| > t_{n-1,\,\alpha/2}\)
  • The two rules always agree, and both agree with the CI: reject exactly when \(\mu_0\) lies outside the \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) interval

The test measures the gap between estimate and claim in standard errors, then asks how unusual that gap would be if the claim were true.

Key Concept 4.4. The example: H0: μ = $40,000 against Ha: μ ≠ $40,000 at α = 0.05.

Code: test \(H_0: \mu = \$40{,}000\)

import numpy as np                          # square roots
import pandas as pd                         # tabular data
from scipy import stats                     # t distribution
url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quarcs-lab/data-open/master/AED/AED_EARNINGS.DTA"
earnings = pd.read_stata(url)["earnings"]   # 171 women, annual earnings
n, mean_earnings = len(earnings), earnings.mean()          # n and x-bar
se_earnings = earnings.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)            # standard error
mu0 = 40000                                 # hypothesized value under H0
t_stat = (mean_earnings - mu0) / se_earnings               # distance in SEs
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.t.cdf(abs(t_stat), n - 1))        # two-sided p-value
t_crit_95 = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1)       # critical value for alpha = 0.05
print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f}, p = {p_value:.4f}, critical = ±{t_crit_95:.4f}")
print("Reject H0" if p_value < 0.05 else "Do not reject H0")   # the decision

What “do not reject” does and does not mean

  • It means: the data are consistent with \(\mu = \$40{,}000\)
  • It does not mean \(\mu = \$40{,}000\): $41,000, $43,000 or $45,000 are consistent too; the CI lists them all
  • The \(p\)-value is not \(\Pr[H_0 \text{ true}]\): it is the probability of a gap at least this large if \(H_0\) were true
  • The result could be a Type II error: a real difference that this sample is too small to detect

Key Concept 4.6: “fail to reject” is not “accept”. Report the confidence interval, which says how large a difference the data still allow.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The interval allows a population mean as much as $5,266 above $40,000.

Two ways to be wrong

\(H_0\) true in reality \(H_0\) false in reality
Reject \(H_0\) Type I error (false alarm), probability \(\alpha\) correct: power \(= 1-\beta\)
Do not reject \(H_0\) correct, probability \(1-\alpha\) Type II error (missed effect), probability \(\beta\)
  • We choose \(\alpha\) (e.g. 0.05); \(\beta\) depends on \(n\), on the true gap, and on \(\alpha\)
  • Lowering \(\alpha\) to 0.01 cuts false alarms but raises \(\beta\); raising it to 0.10 does the reverse
  • Only a larger sample (or a larger true effect) lowers both at once

In the earnings test we did not reject, so the only mistake we could have made is a Type II error.

Choose α by the cost of each error. Medical trials, where a false claim is very costly, use α = 0.01; exploratory work sometimes uses 0.10. The usual target for power is 80% (β = 0.20).

Code: how likely was this test to detect a real gap?

# continues from the previous block (earnings, mu0 defined) — computed for these slides
s = earnings.std(ddof=1)                    # sample std dev, treated as known
for n_size in [171, 684]:                   # our sample, and 4x larger
    se = s / np.sqrt(n_size)                # standard error at this n
    df, c = n_size - 1, stats.t.ppf(0.975, n_size - 1)   # df and 5% critical value
    nc = (41412.69 - mu0) / se              # gap in SEs if the true mean were $41,412.69
    power = stats.nct.sf(c, df, nc) + stats.nct.cdf(-c, df, nc)   # P(|T| > c)
    print(f"n = {n_size}: power = {power:.3f}")   # probability of rejecting H0

Gas prices: are Yolo County prices different from the state’s?

Question. Is the mean price at Yolo County gas stations different from the California average of $3.81 per gallon?

Data. AED_GASPRICE.DTA: 32 stations in three cities (West Sacramento 15, Davis 10, Woodland 7).

\[H_0: \mu = 3.81 \qquad H_a: \mu \neq 3.81 \qquad t \text{ with } 31 \text{ degrees of freedom}\]

A two-sided question: prices could be higher or lower than the state average.

The chapter frames this as the classic statistical-vs-practical significance example. The critical value with 31 degrees of freedom is 2.04, a little above 1.96 because the sample is small.

Code: test the gas-price claim

import numpy as np                          # square roots
import pandas as pd                         # tabular data
from scipy import stats                     # t distribution
url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quarcs-lab/data-open/master/AED/AED_GASPRICE.DTA"
price = pd.read_stata(url)["price"]         # price per gallon at 32 stations
mean_price = price.mean()                   # sample mean
std_price = price.std(ddof=1)               # sample std dev
n_price = len(price)                        # 32 stations

mu0_price = 3.81                            # California state average
t_stat_price = (mean_price - mu0_price) / se_price               # distance in SEs
p_value_price = 2 * (1 - stats.t.cdf(abs(t_stat_price), n_price - 1))   # two-sided p
print(f"mean {mean_price:.4f}, se {se_price:.4f}, t {t_stat_price:.4f}, p {p_value_price:.6f}")

Section 4.6 · Does \(\mu\) exceed $40,000?

One-sided (directional) tests · upper and lower tails

Section 4.6: one-sided tests. Tools: upper- and lower-tailed hypotheses, one-tail p-values and critical values, and when a one-sided test is legitimate.

A directional claim puts the whole rejection region in one tail

Test \(H_0\) \(H_a\) \(p\)-value Reject if
Upper-tailed \(\mu \leq \mu^*\) \(\mu > \mu^*\) \(\Pr[T_{n-1} \geq t]\) \(t > t_{n-1,\,\alpha}\)
Lower-tailed \(\mu \geq \mu^*\) \(\mu < \mu^*\) \(\Pr[T_{n-1} \leq t]\) \(t < -t_{n-1,\,\alpha}\)
Two-sided \(\mu = \mu^*\) \(\mu \neq \mu^*\) \(2\Pr[T_{n-1} \geq \lvert t \rvert]\) \(\lvert t \rvert > t_{n-1,\,\alpha/2}\)
  • The claim we want to establish goes in \(H_a\)
  • All of \(\alpha\) sits in one tail, so the critical value is smaller: 1.654 instead of 1.974 for \(n = 171\)
  • If \(t\) has the sign \(H_a\) predicts, the one-sided \(p\) is half the two-sided \(p\); if not, it is above 0.5

One-sided tests are more powerful in the chosen direction and blind in the other.

Key Concept 4.8. The “half the two-sided p-value” rule holds only when the estimate falls on the side Ha predicts. Case Study 1, Task 4 shows the opposite case.

Code: upper-tailed test of \(H_a: \mu > \$40{,}000\)

import numpy as np                          # square roots
import pandas as pd                         # tabular data
from scipy import stats                     # t distribution
url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quarcs-lab/data-open/master/AED/AED_EARNINGS.DTA"
earnings = pd.read_stata(url)["earnings"]   # 171 women, annual earnings
n, se_earnings = len(earnings), earnings.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(earnings))   # n and SE

t_stat = (earnings.mean() - mu0) / se_earnings     # same t as the two-sided test

print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f}, one-sided p = {p_value_upper:.4f}, critical = {t_crit_upper:.4f}")

When is a one-sided test legitimate?

Use it when

  • theory or policy fixes the direction before seeing the data
  • only one direction would change a decision (a drug better than placebo; a policy that raises income)

Avoid it when

  • either direction would be interesting
  • the field reports two-sided tests (economics usually does)
  • you switch after seeing a two-sided \(p = 0.08\) to get a one-sided \(p = 0.04\): that is \(p\)-hacking

Decide the direction before looking at the data. A one-sided test chosen afterwards has a true false-alarm rate of 10%, not 5%.

If a researcher picks the tail after seeing which way the estimate points, they effectively run a two-sided test at the 10% level while reporting 5%. That is why the choice must be pre-specified.

Section 4.7 · Is the race tied?

Proportions · binary data · the \(z\) test

Section 4.7: proportions. Tools: the sample proportion as a mean of 0/1 data, its standard error, the normal-based CI and z test.

A proportion is just the mean of 0/1 data

\[\hat{p} = \bar{x} = \frac{\#\text{yes}}{n} \qquad \text{se}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \qquad \text{CI: } \hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\text{se}(\hat{p})\]

  • Code each voter 1 (Democrat) or 0 (not): \(\hat{p}\) is the sample mean and the variance is \(\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\)
  • With large \(n\) (rule of thumb: \(n\hat{p} \geq 10\) and \(n(1-\hat{p}) \geq 10\)) use the normal critical value, 1.96
  • To test \(H_0: p = p_0\), use the SE implied by \(H_0\): \(z = (\hat{p} - p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\)

Everything learned about means carries over; only the SE formula changes.

Key Concept 4.9. Two different SEs appear: the CI uses p̂(1 − p̂)/n, the test uses p0(1 − p0)/n because under H0 we know the variance. With p̂ = 0.521 and p0 = 0.5 they are almost identical (0.01646 vs 0.01648).

Code: 480 of 921 voters intend to vote Democrat

import numpy as np                          # square roots
from scipy import stats                     # normal distribution
n_total = 921                               # voters polled
n_success = 480                             # intend to vote Democrat
p_hat = n_success / n_total                 # sample proportion
se_prop = np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n_total)   # SE at p-hat (for the CI)
z_crit = 1.96                               # normal critical value, 95%
ci_lower_prop = p_hat - z_crit * se_prop    # lower bound
ci_upper_prop = p_hat + z_crit * se_prop    # upper bound
p0 = 0.50                                   # H0: tied race
se_under_h0 = np.sqrt(p0 * (1 - p0) / n_total)     # SE implied by H0
z_stat = (p_hat - p0) / se_under_h0         # z statistic
p_value_prop = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z_stat)))   # two-sided p (normal)
print(f"p_hat {p_hat:.4f}, se {se_prop:.4f}")   # estimate and its SE
print(f"95% CI [{ci_lower_prop:.4f}, {ci_upper_prop:.4f}]")   # interval
print(f"z {z_stat:.4f}, p {p_value_prop:.4f}")   # test of a tied race

Caveats on polls

  • The SE covers sampling error only; non-response and people who change their minds are not in it

The tempting mistakes — and the corrections

Objection. “\(p < 0.05\), so the effect is real and important; \(p > 0.05\), so there is no effect.”

Response. Bolivia’s mean IMDS differs “significantly” from 50 by 1.2 points: real but unimportant. Female earnings were not significantly above $40,000, yet the test had an 11% chance of detecting a $1,400 gap: no rejection, but no evidence of no effect either.

Report the estimate and its confidence interval first; the \(p\)-value is a footnote to them.

The chapter’s “Common Mistakes to Avoid”: a p-value is not the probability that H0 is true; statistical significance is not practical importance; a one-sided test needs prior justification.

Five questions to ask of any test

  1. What exactly is \(H_0\), and is the claim of interest in \(H_a\)? Was the direction fixed in advance?
  2. Are the observations independent? Repeated units call for pairing; time series and clusters need other SEs
  3. How big is the gap, in units that matter (dollars, points, percentage points)?
  4. How wide is the CI, and what is the power? Could the test have detected a gap that matters?
  5. How many tests were run? Ten tests at 5% raise the chance of a false alarm to about 40%

These five questions turn a \(p\)-value from a verdict into evidence.

A checklist students can apply to every inference table in the rest of the book. Questions 2 and 4 are the ones the chapter’s own GDP and earnings examples needed most.

What to carry forward

  • The standard error \(s/\sqrt{n}\) drives every interval and test, and it shrinks only with \(\sqrt{n}\)
  • Use the \(t\) distribution when \(\sigma\) is estimated; by \(n \approx 100\) it is close to the normal
  • A 95% CI is the set of values a two-sided 5% test would not reject; read it as a repeated-sampling statement
  • \(t = (\text{estimate} - \text{hypothesized value})/\text{SE}\): the recipe for every test in Chapters 7–17
