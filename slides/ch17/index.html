Where one cross-section runs out

Act I · The tension

This is the capstone chapter. Chapters 1–16 fitted regressions to cross-sections: one observation per unit, no ordering. Real economic data usually has more structure: the same units observed repeatedly (panels), observations ordered in time (time series), and regressors that are themselves chosen (endogeneity). Each structure changes what a slope means and how uncertain it is.

A slope is only as good as the variation behind it

A regression slope compares observations. With one cross-section, the only comparison is across units: big-market teams versus small-market teams, rich countries versus poor ones. Whatever else differs between them rides along in the slope.

This chapter adds two new sources of variation and one warning:

  • Panel data: the same unit over time, so we can compare a unit with itself
  • Time series: one unit, many periods, so we must respect order and memory
  • Causation: no amount of structure helps if the regressor is correlated with the error

The question running through the whole deck: which variation identifies this coefficient, and which standard error is honest about it?

Frame the deck around one question. Pooled OLS uses all variation; fixed effects uses only within-unit variation; first differences uses only period-to-period changes; levels regressions with trending series may be identified by nothing more than two shared trends. The standard error has to match the dependence in the data: clustered for panels, HAC for time series.

Cluster-robust SEs allow any correlation pattern among the observations of one team (or country, or municipality), so they cover both persistent team effects and serial correlation within a team. Newey–West SEs allow correlation that dies out after a chosen number of lags (24 months here).

The thread: which variation identifies the slope?

  • Pooled OLS uses all variation: between units and within units over time
  • Between variation: differences in unit averages (Knicks vs Bucks)
  • Within variation: a unit’s deviations from its own average (the Lakers’ good vs bad seasons). Fixed effects use only this
  • Changes (\(\Delta y_t\) on \(\Delta x_t\)): period-to-period movements. First differences and time-series regressions in changes use only this
  • Levels of two trending series: may be identified by little more than two shared trends, the classic spurious regression

When a coefficient changes as we move from one kind of variation to another, the change itself is evidence about confounding.

This is the deck’s recurring thread. We will see it four times: NBA (0.0068 pooled → 0.0045 within), countries (capital elasticity 0.71 → 0.44), Bolivian municipalities (population slope +0.34 → −0.82), and interest rates (levels vs changes). Each change is a diagnostic for confounding by something time-invariant (panels) or by a common trend (time series).

Six ideas anchor this chapter

  • Panel data: observations indexed by unit \(i\) and time \(t\)
  • Within estimator: subtract each unit’s own mean to erase its fixed traits \(\alpha_i\)
  • Logit: push \(x'\beta\) through an S-curve so probabilities stay in \([0,1]\)
  • AR(\(p\)): regress \(y_t\) on its own past values; persistence and momentum
  • ADL: add current and lagged \(x\); cumulative sums of the \(\gamma\)’s trace the dynamic effect
  • Hausman test: compares FE and RE; a significant gap rejects RE’s assumption

Each idea answers the same question under harder conditions: what is really driving \(y\)?

These are the chapter’s Key Concepts. One caution: the Hausman test appears in the Key Concepts and Key Takeaways, but the chapter never estimates random effects or runs the test. The deck presents it as a concept (17.4), not as a result.

The question and the panel

Data. NBA team finances, 2001–02 to 2010–11 (AED_NBA.DTA): 29 teams, 286 team-seasons. Revenue is in millions of dollars.

The panel is unbalanced: 28 teams appear in all 10 seasons, the Bobcats in only 6, so \(N = 286\), not \(29 \times 10 = 290\).

Economic question: does winning raise a team’s revenue? A team that wins more sells more tickets, merchandise and playoff games. But big-market teams also have more money to buy winning rosters, so the association could run both ways.

The chapter’s cluster-SE discussion says “29 teams × 10 years = 290 independent observations”; the data have 286.

Three ways to use a panel

\[y_{it} = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x_{it} + u_{it}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n \;(\text{teams}),\; t = 1,\dots,T \;(\text{seasons})\]

  • Pooled OLS: ignore the panel structure in estimation; fix the SEs by clustering on \(i\)
  • Fixed effects (FE): give each unit its own intercept, \(y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_2 x_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}\), and estimate \(\beta_2\) from within-unit changes only
  • Random effects (RE): treat \(\alpha_i\) as a random draw, \(u_{it} = \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it}\), assumed uncorrelated with \(x\); uses a weighted mix of within and between variation

The three estimators differ in which comparison they make, not in arithmetic sophistication.

Pooled OLS treats each team-season as a separate observation for the coefficient but must cluster for inference. FE allows the team effect to be correlated with wins (big markets win more) because it never compares one team with another. RE is more efficient but only consistent if the team effect is unrelated to the regressor.

Why clustering: ten seasons of one team are not ten independent facts

\[\widehat{V}_{\text{CR}}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^{-1}\Big(\textstyle\sum_{g=1}^{G} X_g'\hat u_g \hat u_g' X_g\Big)(X'X)^{-1}\]

  • The middle term sums over clusters \(g\), so every pair of residuals within a team may be correlated; residuals in different teams are assumed independent
  • Two sources of within-team correlation: a persistent team effect (the Lakers’ residual is positive every year) and serial correlation (good years follow good years)
  • In the limit of perfect correlation, 10 seasons of one team carry the information of 1 observation; the truth lies in between

Clustering must be done at the level where shocks are shared. With \(G = 29\) clusters the \(t\) reference has only \(G - 1 = 28\) degrees of freedom.

The chapter writes the cluster formula with an ellipsis in the denominator; the sandwich form above is the complete version. Practical cautions from the chapter: with fewer than about 30 clusters, cluster-robust SEs can still be too small; the wild cluster bootstrap is the usual refinement (mentioned, not run). Two-way clustering (team and season) allows common season shocks too; its variance is V_team + V_season − V_team×season.

17.3 · Fixed effects: compare each team only with itself

Within transformation · LSDV · what FE absorbs · omitted-variable bias

Section 17.3. Fixed effects remove everything about a team that does not change over the decade: market size, arena, brand, ownership, local income (to the extent it is stable).

The fixed-effects model and the within transformation

\[y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta\,x_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}\]

Average each team’s equation over its seasons and subtract:

\[(y_{it}-\bar y_i) = \beta\,(x_{it}-\bar x_i) + (\varepsilon_{it}-\bar\varepsilon_i)\]

  • \(\alpha_i\) disappears: anything constant within a team cannot bias \(\hat\beta\), even if it is correlated with \(x\)
  • Same estimate two ways: LSDV (one dummy per team) or de-meaning then OLS
  • Price: coefficients on time-invariant regressors cannot be estimated, and only within variation is used

FE swap the assumption “no omitted variables” for the weaker “no omitted variables that change over time within a unit”.

This is the key identifying assumption, often called strict exogeneity: the time-varying error must be uncorrelated with the regressor in every period. Team traits that are fixed over ten seasons (market size, arena, history) are absorbed by alpha_i. Anything that changes over time and drives both wins and revenue (a new star, a new arena, a new TV contract) is not.

Code: fixed effects, and a check by hand

# continues from the previous block
fit_fe = pf.feols("lnrevenue ~ wins | teamid", data=data_nba,
                  vcov={"CRV1": "teamid"})   # "| teamid" = one intercept per team
fit_fe.summary()                             # within estimator, cluster SEs by team
print(fit_fe._r2_within)                     # share of WITHIN-team variation explained
# check: the same slope from OLS on de-meaned data (the within transformation)

data_nba["dm_wins"] = data_nba["wins"] - g["wins"].transform("mean")

Why the slope fell: omitted-variable bias, signed

\[\text{plim}\;\hat\beta_{\text{pooled}} = \beta + \gamma\,\frac{\text{Cov}(\alpha_i, x_{it})}{\text{Var}(x_{it})}\]

  • \(\gamma > 0\): the team effect (market, brand) raises revenue
  • \(\text{Cov}(\alpha_i, x_{it}) > 0\): rich-market teams also win somewhat more (team-average revenue and wins correlate 0.40)
  • So the pooled slope is biased upward: 0.00675 vs 0.00451, a gap of 0.00225

The direction of the change from pooled to FE is exactly what the omitted-variables formula predicts when the omitted trait helps both \(x\) and \(y\).

Same formula as the Phillips-curve case in Chapter 13: bias = (effect of omitted variable on y) × (slope of omitted variable on x). Here the omitted variable is the team fixed effect. FE does not estimate gamma; it removes the term by construction. The 0.40 correlation is between the 29 team averages of log revenue and wins (computed for these slides).

What one more win is worth

  • Per win: \(100\,(e^{0.00451}-1) = 0.45\%\) more revenue for the same team
  • At the sample mean revenue of $95.7M: about $0.43M per win
  • Ten more wins than the team’s usual: \(100\,(e^{10 \times 0.00451}-1) = 4.6\%\), about $4.4M
  • The pooled slope would have said 7.0% ($6.7M) for ten wins

Winning pays, but far less than the cross-section suggests. Market size, not the win column, is where most revenue differences come from.

The chapter evaluates the effect “for a team with $200M revenue” (0.45% × $200M = $900K). No team-season in this sample reaches $200M (maximum $187.7M; mean $95.7M), so the deck evaluates it at the sample mean instead. All conversions use the exact exponential formula.

What fixed effects can and cannot fix

Absorbed (time-invariant)

  • market size and local income level
  • arena, franchise history, brand
  • ownership style (if stable)

Not absorbed (time-varying)

  • a star arrives, a coach changes, injuries
  • new arena or TV contract mid-panel
  • league-wide shocks (lockouts, recessions)
  • revenue → payroll → wins (reverse causality)

FE make the causal reading more plausible, not guaranteed. A natural next step is season fixed effects, | teamid + season, for league-wide shocks.

17.4 · Fixed or random effects?

Random effects · efficiency vs consistency · the Hausman test

Section 17.4. The chapter describes random effects and the Hausman test but does not estimate either: its 17.4 code prints the pooled-vs-FE comparison. The deck therefore reviews the method and reports what the code actually computes.

Random effects: a team effect assumed unrelated to wins

\[y_{it} = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x_{it} + \underbrace{(\alpha_i + \varepsilon_{it})}_{\text{composite error}}, \qquad \alpha_i \sim (0,\sigma_\alpha^2),\;\; \varepsilon_{it} \sim (0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2)\]

  • Key assumption: \(\text{Cov}(\alpha_i, x_{it}) = 0\), i.e. the team effect is unrelated to the regressor
  • Estimation: feasible GLS, which partially de-means: \(y_{it} - \theta\,\bar y_i\) with \(\theta = 1 - \sqrt{\sigma_\varepsilon^2 / (\sigma_\varepsilon^2 + T\sigma_\alpha^2)}\)
  • \(\theta = 0\) gives pooled OLS, \(\theta = 1\) gives FE; RE sits in between and uses some between variation

RE buys efficiency with an assumption. If big-market teams also win more, the assumption fails and RE inherits part of the pooled bias.

The quasi-demeaning formula is the standard textbook RE (GLS) transformation, added here as a method review; the chapter states only “Feasible GLS”. With T = 10 seasons and a large team-effect variance, theta would be close to 1, so RE would land close to FE. That is a statement about the formula, not an estimate: RE is not estimated in the chapter or in this deck.

Pooled, RE, FE: what each one compares

Pooled OLS Random effects Fixed effects
Variation used between + within, equal weights within + a fraction \((1-\theta)\) of between within only
Assumes about \(\alpha_i\) uncorrelated with \(x\) (and ignored) uncorrelated with \(x\) nothing; may correlate with \(x\)
Consistent if \(\alpha_i\) correlates with \(x\)? no no yes
Efficient if it doesn’t? no yes no
Time-invariant regressors? yes yes no
NBA estimate 0.00675 not estimated 0.00451

In observational panels the “no correlation” assumption is rarely credible, so FE is the default and RE needs a justification.

RE is typically defensible when the unit effects are plausibly random with respect to the regressor, for example when the regressor is randomly assigned. In the NBA case, market size almost certainly correlates with the ability to buy a winning roster.

The Hausman test: does RE’s assumption survive?

\[H = (\hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE})'\,\big[\widehat V(\hat\beta_{FE}) - \widehat V(\hat\beta_{RE})\big]^{-1}(\hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE}) \;\sim\; \chi^2_k\]

  • \(H_0\): \(\alpha_i\) uncorrelated with the regressors, so both are consistent and RE is efficient: the two estimates should be close
  • \(H_a\): correlated, so only FE is consistent: the estimates diverge
  • A large \(H\) (small \(p\)) rejects RE; keep FE

The chapter states the test but never computes it, so this deck reports no Hausman result for the NBA. Treat any claim that “Hausman rejects RE here” as unverified.

The classic Hausman statistic assumes RE is fully efficient under H0, which fails with heteroskedastic or clustered errors; a robust alternative is the correlated-random-effects (Mundlak) test, adding team means of x as regressors. Both are mentioned as standard practice, not estimated.

Fidelity note: the chapter’s Key Concepts example says the FE coefficient is “noticeably smaller than the RE coefficient” and that the Hausman test “typically rejects RE”. Neither RE nor the test is estimated anywhere in the chapter. Practice Exercise 3 (χ² = 25.4) is a hypothetical, not this dataset.

  • Linear probability model (LPM): \(\Pr(y=1\mid x) = x'\beta\); slopes are probability changes, but fitted values can leave \([0,1]\)

The question and the data

Economic question: how much of a move in short rates (which the Fed influences) passes through to long rates (which matter for mortgages and investment)?

The expectations hypothesis says long rates reflect expected future short rates, plus a term premium. If so, short-rate moves should pass through to long rates, but less than one-for-one, because a 1-year move changes only part of the expected 10-year path.

Three time-series ideas to keep in view

  • Autocorrelation at lag \(k\): \(r_k = \dfrac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)(y_{t-k}-\bar y)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)^2}\). Today resembles yesterday
  • Stationarity: mean, variance and autocorrelations do not change over time. Trending series are non-stationary
  • Spurious regression: regress one trending series on another and you get a high \(R^2\) and huge \(t\)-statistics even with no structural link

With trending data a large \(R^2\) is a warning, not a reward. Two cures: difference the data, or keep levels and use HAC standard errors (and think hard).

Many macro series behave like random walks with drift; their levels wander and their changes are roughly stationary. Spurious regression (Granger and Newbold) is the time-series analogue of confounding by a common factor: here the common factor is time itself.

Both rates drift down from 14% to near zero

Monthly 10-year and 1-year Treasury yields, 1982–2015.

Over 33 years both rates fall by more than ten points. Any regression of one level on the other will partly fit this shared downward drift. In levels the two series correlate 0.95.

Notice also the late period: after 2008 the 1-year rate is pinned near zero (the zero lower bound) while the 10-year rate keeps moving. A single linear relationship across 1982–2015 also assumes the relationship is stable through very different monetary regimes.

How HAC standard errors work

\[\widehat V_{\text{HAC}} = (X'X)^{-1}\Big[\hat\Omega_0 + \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} w_\ell\,\big(\hat\Omega_\ell + \hat\Omega_\ell'\big)\Big](X'X)^{-1}, \qquad w_\ell = 1 - \frac{\ell}{L+1}\]

  • \(\hat\Omega_\ell = \sum_t \hat u_t \hat u_{t-\ell}\, x_t x_{t-\ell}'\): the covariance of scores \(\ell\) months apart
  • Bartlett weights \(w_\ell\) shrink distant lags; \(L\) is chosen by the analyst (here 24 months)
  • HAC fixes the standard errors; it does not fix a spurious coefficient

HAC is to time series what clustering is to panels: same sandwich, different idea of which errors may be correlated.

The Newey–West estimator is the cluster-robust sandwich with a different middle: instead of summing within clusters, it sums cross-products of nearby periods with declining weights. Choosing L: rules of thumb such as 0.75·T^(1/3) give about 5.5 for T = 397; the chapter chooses 24 (two years) to be conservative given the strong persistence.

17.6 · Autocorrelation: is the levels fit spurious?

Correlogram · consequences · first differencing

Autocorrelation: what it breaks and how to see it

  • Consequences: OLS coefficients stay unbiased (if the regressors are exogenous), but default SEs are wrong, usually too small, and \(t\)-tests over-reject
  • Detect: the correlogram of residuals; the Breusch–Godfrey LM test; the Durbin–Watson statistic for AR(1) errors

\[\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}, \qquad \Delta\text{gs10}_t = \beta_1 + \beta_2\,\Delta\text{gs1}_t + u_t\]

  • The question changes: “when short rates move this month, how much do long rates move?”
  • Cost: long-run level information is discarded; only month-to-month variation identifies \(\beta_2\)

The time-series cousin of fixed effects: drop the persistent component, keep the changes.

The analogy is exact for two-period panels: first differences and fixed effects coincide when T = 2. In both cases, a component that does not change (a team’s market, a series’ level) is removed so that it cannot confound the slope.

17.6b · How fast do short-rate moves reach long rates?

AR(\(p\)) · ADL(\(p,q\)) · impact and cumulative multipliers

Still section 17.6 in the chapter (Autoregressive Models). The ADL(2,2) is estimated in the chapter’s Key Takeaways code block; the deck runs it as a main result.

Dynamic models: let the past enter the regression

\[\text{AR}(p):\quad y_t = \beta_0 + \alpha_1 y_{t-1} + \cdots + \alpha_p y_{t-p} + u_t\]

\[\text{ADL}(p,q):\quad y_t = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_j\,y_{t-j} + \sum_{j=0}^{q}\gamma_j\,x_{t-j} + u_t\]

  • \(\alpha_j\): persistence (momentum or reversal) in \(y\) itself
  • \(\gamma_0\): impact multiplier, the same-month response to \(x\)
  • \(\gamma_0+\gamma_1+\dots\): cumulative distributed-lag multipliers; the long-run multiplier also accounts for the AR terms: \(\sum_j\gamma_j \,/\, (1-\sum_j \alpha_j)\)

Here \(y_t = \Delta\text{gs10}_t\) and \(x_t = \Delta\text{gs1}_t\): an ADL(2,2) in changes.

The long-run multiplier formula is standard for stable ADL models: after a permanent unit change in x, y settles where y = (Σγ/(1−Σα))·x. With y and x in changes, this is the long-run response of the change in the long rate to a sustained change in the change of the short rate. Students should mainly read the impact and cumulative sums.

Reading time-series regressions with care

  • Units: changes are in percentage points, not percent. “A 1% rise in the 1-year rate” should read “a 1-percentage-point rise”
  • Levels vs changes answer different questions: equilibrium co-movement vs monthly pass-through
  • Stability: one set of coefficients spans 1982–2015, including the zero-lower-bound years after 2008
  • Not causal by default: both rates respond to the same news (inflation, growth); the ADL describes predictive dynamics and transmission, not a controlled experiment

A time-series regression is credible when its residuals are white noise and its story survives a check of stability and common shocks.

17.7 · When is a slope causal?

Concepts only · worked designs in the Chapter 13 deck

Bridge from the panel and time-series sections: FE removed time-invariant confounders and differencing removed trends, but neither guarantees that the remaining variation in x is as good as random.

OLS is biased the moment \(x\) is correlated with the error

\[y = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x + u, \qquad \text{OLS is consistent only if } E[u \mid x] = 0\]

  • Omitted variables: a confounder drives both \(x\) and \(y\). NBA: market size raises revenue and buys wins (FE removed the time-invariant part)
  • Measurement error in \(x\): attenuates the slope toward zero
  • Simultaneity / reverse causation: \(y\) also moves \(x\). NBA: revenue pays for players, players produce wins

Adding controls fixes only the confounders you measure. Credible causal claims need an identification strategy.

Each source has a different direction of bias. Omitted variables can go either way (sign of the OVB formula); classical measurement error biases toward zero; simultaneity depends on the feedback. Fixed effects address only one specific form of omitted variable: the time-invariant one.

Potential outcomes: what a causal effect means

\[\text{individual effect} = Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}, \qquad \text{ATE} = E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}]\]

  • \(Y_{1i}\): the outcome if treated; \(Y_{0i}\): the outcome if not. Only one is ever observed
  • A naive comparison of treated and untreated: \(E[Y_1\mid D{=}1] - E[Y_0\mid D{=}0] = \text{ATT} + \underbrace{E[Y_0\mid D{=}1]-E[Y_0\mid D{=}0]}_{\text{selection bias}}\)
  • Every design is a way to make the selection-bias term zero, or to remove it

A causal estimate is a claim about a counterfactual. The design is the argument for why your comparison group stands in for it.

The chapter defines potential outcomes and the ATE; the decomposition into ATT plus selection bias is the standard result (added as method review, and worked through with data in the Chapter 13 deck’s Act II-b).

Instrumental variables: borrow variation that is as good as random

An instrument \(z\) must satisfy two conditions:

  • Relevance: \(\text{Cov}(z, x) \neq 0\). Testable: first-stage \(F\) (rule of thumb \(> 10\))
  • Exogeneity / exclusion: \(\text{Cov}(z, u) = 0\), so \(z\) affects \(y\) only through \(x\). Not testable: must be argued

With one regressor and one instrument, substitute \(y = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x + u\) into \(\text{Cov}(z, y)\):

\[\text{Cov}(z,y) = \beta_2\,\text{Cov}(z,x) + \underbrace{\text{Cov}(z,u)}_{=\,0} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \hat\beta_{IV} = \frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}(z,y)}{\widehat{\text{Cov}}(z,x)} = \frac{\text{reduced form}}{\text{first stage}}\]

IV is consistent where OLS is not, but only for the variation the instrument moves (a local effect), and only as good as the exclusion argument.

The derivation shows exactly where each condition enters: exogeneity kills the Cov(z,u) term; relevance makes the denominator non-zero. A weak first stage (small Cov(z,x)) magnifies any small violation of exogeneity. In pyfixest the syntax is pf.feols(“y ~ 1 | x ~ z”, data=…); a worked example with data is in the Chapter 13 deck (case 13.8).

Task 3 · Interpretation: what the sign flip does and does not mean

  • In total lights: \(-0.82 + 1 = 0.18\). When a municipality’s population grows faster than its own trend, its total light grows much less than proportionally, so light per person falls

Task 5 · FE vs FD: same idea, different weights

  • Both remove \(\alpha_i\). FE compares each year with the municipality’s 9-year average; FD compares each year with the previous one
  • With \(T > 2\) they differ when errors are serially correlated or the timing of effects matters: FE is efficient with serially uncorrelated errors, FD with random-walk errors
  • Here: FE −0.82 (\(p = 0.005\)), FD −0.52 (\(p = 0.076\)); same sign, overlapping CIs

Both point the same way, but FD cannot reject zero at 5%. The population slope is the weakest link in this case study.

Total-lights elasticities implied: pooled 1.34, FE 0.18, FD 0.48. When FE and FD diverge, it often signals measurement error (differencing amplifies noise relative to signal) or dynamics; both apply here, given how little population moves within municipalities.

Choosing an estimator: a short guide

  • Unit traits drive both \(x\) and \(y\)? Fixed effects (plus time effects); cluster by unit
  • Unit effects unrelated to \(x\)? Random effects; check with Hausman or Mundlak
  • Trending time series? Check the correlogram; use changes or AR/ADL dynamics; HAC SEs
  • Binary outcome? LPM for averages; logit for probabilities, with marginal effects
  • Time-varying confounders or feedback? You need a design: IV, DiD, RD, RCT

Start from the structure of the data and the likely confounders, then pick the estimator, then the standard error.

A decision guide, not an algorithm. Many applied papers combine elements: two-way FE with clustered SEs is the default panel specification in much of applied economics, and DiD is two-way FE with a treatment indicator.

The tempting mistake — and the correction

Objection. “Fixed effects control for unobserved heterogeneity, so the FE coefficient is causal.”

Response. FE remove only what is constant within a unit. In the NBA a new star raises wins and revenue in the same season; in Bolivia population and lights co-move with whatever drives local growth. And FE throw away the between variation, so the estimate rests on the within variation that remains: 69% of the variance of wins, but only 0.4% of the variance of municipal population.

FE are a design with an assumption (no time-varying confounders) and a cost (less variation). Check both before saying “effect”.

Steelman the objection: FE do solve a large class of omitted-variable problems, and the move from pooled to FE is often the single most informative robustness check in a panel. But “unobserved heterogeneity” in the FE sense means time-invariant heterogeneity only.

Five questions to ask of any panel or time-series coefficient

  1. Which variation? Between, within, changes, or two shared trends?
  2. Which units? Log points, percent, percentage points, probability, odds?
  3. Which standard error? Clustered at the level where shocks are shared, or HAC over the right horizon?
  4. What is held fixed? Unit effects, time effects, a trend, lagged values?
  5. What could still move both \(x\) and \(y\)? Time-varying confounders, feedback, common shocks?

The unit errors corrected in this deck: rate changes in percentage points (not %), logit coefficients as log-odds (not probabilities), and log-point effects converted with \(e^b - 1\).

What to carry forward

  • Panels: decompose the variation; FE use only the within part; cluster by unit

  • Logit: report marginal effects, and say AME or MEM
  • Time series: high \(R^2\) in levels is a warning; check residual autocorrelation
